About Prayer Times in Indonesia

Indonesia — home to the world's largest Muslim population — predominantly uses the Kementerian Agama (Kemenag) method, with Fajr at 20° and Isha at 18° below the horizon — an earlier Fajr than the Muslim World League’s 18°, which is why imported apps often disagree with the local jadwal. Asr follows the Shafi'i calculation (shadow = object length), consistent with the dominant madhhab in the region.

The Qibla from Indonesia points roughly northwest — about 295° from Jakarta, Bandung and Surabaya alike. During Ramadan, sahur ends at Fajr (Imsak is traditionally called 10 minutes earlier) and buka puasa begins at Maghrib. Azan Time shows live countdowns for both.

How the cities in Indonesia compare

Earliest and latest Fajr and Maghrib across the year (standard time) and the great-circle qibla bearing, calculated for each city’s coordinates with the method shown on its page. On 15 June a fast from Fajr to Maghrib lasts 13 h 10 min in Jakarta but 13 h 05 min in Surabaya — a difference of 5 minutes inside one country. Dhuhr on 15 March is at 11:38 in Surabaya and 12:02 in Jakarta: the same prayer, 24 minutes apart on the clock, because of where each city sits in its time zone.

Questions about prayer times in Indonesia

When is the longest fast in Indonesia?

Around the June solstice in the northern hemisphere, but here the long days fall in December. In Jakarta, a fast from Fajr to Maghrib on 15 December lasts 13 h 54 min; on 15 June it lasts 13 h 10 min. Ramadan moves about 11 days earlier each year, so the fast length you meet depends on the year.

Which way is the Qibla from Indonesia?

Between 294° and 295.2° from true north, depending on the city (see the table). These are true-north bearings; a phone compass shows magnetic north unless it corrects for declination.

Which calculation method is used for Indonesia?

Kementerian Agama (Kemenag), with the standard Asr time. Each city page states how many minutes apart the standard and Hanafi Asr times are, and names its method.
